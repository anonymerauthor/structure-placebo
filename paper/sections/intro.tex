\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A large and growing class of population-based optimizers is justified
structurally. Candidate solutions are placed on a graph, a statistic of that
graph---centrality, degree, bridge potential---is computed, and the update is
weighted so that agents learn preferentially from neighbours the statistic
distinguishes. The accompanying argument is causal: the method performs well
\emph{because} the structure identifies informative agents. Where such
mechanisms are evaluated at all, it is by deletion---the term is removed and
performance is compared.

Deletion cannot establish the claim. Removing a weighting changes the
optimizer's mixing operator, its effective neighbourhood, its convergence rate
and often its cost, so a difference in outcome is consistent with the weights
having been merely unequal. The claim actually being advanced is narrower and
sharper: that the \emph{assignment} of scores to graph positions, and thereby
to the solutions those positions hold, is informative. Testing it requires
holding the mechanism at full strength while destroying only that
correspondence.

This paper supplies such a test, characterises it mathematically, and applies
it at scale. The instrument is a pair of controls. The first permutes the
structural scores across nodes: the score multiset, the weight heterogeneity
and the arithmetic cost are preserved exactly, while the pairing of score to
position is destroyed. We show (Proposition~\ref{prop:mean}) that this control
has the uniform operator as its expectation while retaining the original's
weight dispersion in every realisation---so it interpolates precisely between
the two alternatives an ablation confounds. The second control applies the same
logic to adaptive components, replacing an informed selection rule by a uniform
draw over the same alternatives on the same schedule, thereby separating
\emph{selecting} a component from merely \emph{varying} it.

A control is informative only if the mechanism it neutralises was doing
something, and we make that precondition quantitative. Proposition~\ref{prop:leverage}
bounds the displacement a structural weighting induces by the product of a
structural leverage term, measuring how concentrated the weights are, and a
populational dispersion term. Reporting this displacement alongside every
comparison distinguishes the uninteresting case of an inert mechanism from the
finding we in fact report: a mechanism that moves the search substantially and
whose control reproduces it exactly.

Applied to a recently published centrality-weighted small-world
optimizer~\cite{jalali2026social}, the permutation control is indistinguishable
from the published method on every function of three suites, at three budgets,
under Holm-corrected paired tests with effect-size intervals---while the same
mechanism displaces each update by roughly a quarter to a third of the
population's own spread. The structure is doing measurable work of no
informational value.

A null result is worth only as much as the test's ability to return a non-null
one, so we calibrate. The same controls, unchanged, are applied to two further
mechanisms of the same optimizer: the fitness-influence weighting, whose score
is informative by construction, and an adaptive density scheme of our own. The
first yields an intermediate, problem-class-dependent effect reaching
$\delta=-0.80$ on one function; the second a clear one, $\bar{\delta}=-0.101$
at $p=1.5\times10^{-5}$ over $24$ of $28$ functions. Null, intermediate, clear,
on one suite with one statistic: the instrument discriminates, so the null on
centrality is a property of that mechanism and not of the test.

Turning the instrument on our own proposal also corrected our own reading of
it. Judged by per-function corrected tests alone the adaptive scheme looks
weak, winning on $3$ of $28$; judged at suite level it is unambiguous. We
report both, and note the asymmetry we would otherwise have introduced by
applying a stringent test to a published method and a lenient one to ourselves.

\subsection*{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Two controls for structural attribution}, defined for the general
  mixing-operator form \eqref{eq:master} that subsumes cellular evolutionary
  algorithms, neighbourhood-topology swarms, localized consensus dynamics and
  centrality-weighted methods. They are drop-in, budget-neutral, and require no
  modification to the optimizer under test.

\item \textbf{Invariance results that make the controls sound.}
  Proposition~\ref{prop:mean} establishes that the permutation control equals
  the uniform operator in expectation while preserving weight heterogeneity
  pathwise, isolating assignment from heterogeneity---the confound that renders
  ablation inconclusive.

\item \textbf{A leverage bound} (Proposition~\ref{prop:leverage}) separating the
  displacement a structural weighting induces into a structural and a
  populational factor, giving a necessary condition for a control comparison to
  be informative and identifying the degenerate topologies on which such
  mechanisms are inert by construction.

\item \textbf{A large-scale negative result.} Across the classic
  $F_{1\text{--}23}$ suite at two budgets and CEC2017 at $D\in\{10,30,50\}$,
  totalling more than $2\times10^{4}$ runs, betweenness-weighted learning is
  statistically indistinguishable from its permutation control on every
  function tested, with effect-size intervals excluding non-negligible effects.

\item \textbf{Identification of what does survive.} A $7{,}350$-run density
  sweep shows neighbourhood size to be consequential in $30$ of $49$ problem
  configurations, with the optimum both problem- and dimension-dependent and
  only weakly predictable from landscape ruggedness---evidence that density
  must be adapted rather than fixed by a topology prior, and simultaneously
  evidence against predicting it in advance.

\item \textbf{Calibration of the instrument.} The same controls, unchanged in
  suite, sample size and statistic, are applied to four further mechanisms
  across two optimizers and return five distinguishable outcomes: inert by
  construction, uninformative, sign-varying by problem class, uniformly
  harmful, and uniformly beneficial ($\bar{\delta}=-0.101$,
  $p=1.5\times10^{-5}$). The null on the centrality assignment therefore cannot
  be attributed to a test without power.

\item \textbf{A second published target, and a reporting lesson.} On the fully
  informed particle swarm, the published quality weighting produces
  per-function effects reaching $|\delta|=1$ in both directions, significant on
  $15$ of $28$ functions, which cancel in the suite mean. A suite average alone
  would call this and the centrality null the same thing; per-function
  correction alone hid a real effect in our own adaptive scheme. Both failures
  are demonstrated here on data rather than argued in principle.

\item \textbf{Self-application.} The adaptive density scheme we propose is
  subjected to the second control on the same terms; we report that it passes,
  and also that our own first reading of it---based on per-function corrected
  tests alone---understated the effect.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{What this paper does not claim}

We do not claim that the optimizer studied performs poorly, that structural
information is useless in principle, or that no centrality-weighted method can
work. The claim is narrower and, we believe, more useful: for the mechanisms
tested here, the evidence offered for structural attribution does not survive a
control that leaves the mechanism running. Whether other structural statistics,
other graphs, or landscape-derived topologies fare differently is an open
question that the same instrument can settle, and
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} sketches how.
