\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Structure-aware metaheuristics are justified by a causal claim---that a
structural quantity computed on the population graph carries information about
where to search---which the usual evidence, an ablation, cannot establish. An
ablation deletes the component and so changes the optimizer's mixing operator
along with the information it carries; the resulting difference is consistent
with the weights having been merely unequal.

We defined two controls that keep the mechanism running at full strength and
destroy only the information it is claimed to use, and characterised them.
Permuting a structural score across nodes yields an operator that equals
uniform mixing in expectation while retaining the original's weight
heterogeneity in every realisation (Proposition~\ref{prop:mean}), which is
exactly the separation an ablation fails to make. A leverage bound
(Proposition~\ref{prop:leverage}) states when such a comparison is informative
at all, factoring the displacement a weighting induces into a structural and a
populational term, and identifying the topologies on which the mechanism is
inert by construction.

Applied to a published betweenness-weighted small-world optimizer, the controls
give a clear answer. The mechanism is active, displacing each update by a
quarter to a third of the population's spread. Its assignment of scores to
graph positions is not: across $104$ function comparisons on two suites, three
dimensions and five budgets, permutation changes nothing that survives
correction, and at suite level the effect is statistically equivalent to zero
within a margin four times stricter than the negligibility threshold.
Separating the two properties an ablation confounds shows why: unequal weights
help slightly, on $3$ of $28$ functions, while which neighbour receives the
large weight matters on none.

A null result of this kind is only as good as the test's ability to return a
non-null one, so we calibrated the instrument on two further mechanisms of the
same optimizer, unchanged in suite, sample size and statistic. On the
fitness-influence weighting it returns an intermediate, class-dependent effect,
reaching $\delta=-0.80$ on a single composition function; on adaptive density
selection it returns a clear one, $\bar{\delta}=-0.101$ at
$p=1.5\times10^{-5}$. The ordering null--intermediate--clear is what a working
instrument should produce, and it forecloses the reading that our centrality
result reflects a test without power.

What carries information in this algorithm, then, is not the scores computed on
the graph but the graph's coarse density: consequential in $30$ of $49$
configurations, with large effects where present, and with an optimum that is
problem- and dimension-dependent and resists prediction from a landscape
ruggedness descriptor. We built an adaptive scheme on that basis and subjected
it to the second control, which it passes.

The instrument costs one arm and no additional budget. We would encourage its
use wherever a structural or adaptive claim is made, with two conditions that
our own analysis makes concrete: report the displacement, so that a control on
an inert mechanism is not mistaken for evidence; and test equivalence
explicitly with adequate power, rather than resting on a non-significant
difference. The most direct extension is to widen the audit---the same controls
apply unchanged to any score-proportional weighting---and, on the constructive
side, to ask whether a graph derived from the landscape rather than fixed in
advance would make a structural statistic informative. The prediction, which
these controls can test, is that on such a graph permutation would hurt.
